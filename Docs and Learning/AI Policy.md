# Generative AI (genAI) Policy and Guidelines

 

We are in PIE at a time when Generative AI tools (such as Claude and ChatGPT) are becoming more capable and pervasive in and around Olin. We want to be on the cutting edge at Olin and embrace new learning ecosystems, but don't want to be on the bleeding edge where students get (academically) hurt through GenAI use.

 

Many professors at Olin and beyond are grappling with how to embrace the positive parts of GenAI and install appropriate guardrails to minimize the negative aspects of its use. We are building our starting point GenAI policies and guidelines informed by many others - and hope to learn from PIE students during each assignment so that we can make updates that make sense for us between each major assignment (if needed). Consider this a collaborative living document.

 

Similar to how math-related educators had to grapple with calculators becoming available, we want to arrive at usage policies for GenAI that turn the "AI" part into "Augmenting (your) Intelligence" and not "Amputating (your) intelligence". There are some people studying the degree to which amputating intelligence happens. Our colleagues who teach ModSIM remind students in their policy about a study of high school students that found: those who used ChatGPT actually learned less than their peers who didn't (Bastani et al. 2024). Other studies showed ChatGPT to be associated with procrastination and memory loss (Abbas et al. 2024). A final study we'll highlight found that students who used ChatGPT to help write essays had significantly lower brain engagement than those who did not have access to the tool (Kosmyna et al., 2025). These phenomena could be chalked up to students using genAI to "think" for them - not our goal here.

 

As we discuss these GenAI tools, we refer to systems that go well beyond ChatGPT, but we can't be exhaustive. Some of the more household name tools, such as Claude (officially supported by Olin), Gemini, Perplexity, and DALL-E also apply.

 

In the spirit of helping you do integrated engineering thinking for yourself, we highlight a limited set of acceptable use cases and counterexamples to illustrate some scenarios you might see this semester. (It is also important to note that what you do in every assignment in PIE affects at least one other person, so you're not just considering these guidelines for yourself, but also for others that are connected to you and your work.)

 

Acceptable GenAI uses:

    Using it to explain code to you—this keeps you thinking about what the code means.
    Using it to generate small examples of code (say, a few lines) that you then adapt for your own use—this keeps you thinking about how to adapt code for your own use.
    Using it to find and explain mistakes in computer code—this is like asking a CA or instructor for help understanding how code went wrong.
    Using it to suggest improvements to draft write-ups you generate—this provides you with the opportunity to express your thoughts in writing, while working through how best to make your thoughts legible and clear to others.
    Using it to assist your CAD efforts—GenAI is far from being good at creating physical systems, so be very cautious about any AI-based tools.

The following are unacceptable uses of GenAI. Note that this list is not exhaustive:

    Using it to generate large sections of code (say, an entire script).
    Having it generate your circuit diagrams from scratch.
    Using it to generate large sections of a write-up that you do not edit (say, an entire paragraph).
    Using it to generate data / figures.
    Using it to generate entire mechanical components (although, if you manage to do this, Georgia would be interested in seeing what you did!).

 

If you use GenAI in any assignment that has a written component, you must write a short disclosure statement describing how you used genAI (if at all) in your work. Not

disclosing your use of genAI is an unacceptable use of GenAI that will adversily affect your grade for the assignment or the professionalism part of your grade.

 

If you are unsure whether a given use of genAI is unacceptable, please talk to your studio instructor (note: not a Course Assistant). Serious breaches of these policies and guidelines may result in an Honor Board situation for whichever campus the student enrolls from.
